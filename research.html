Alkali–silica reaction

The alkali–silica reaction forms layered silicate products whose growth in confined pores damages concrete. We build atomic models of crystalline ASR products (shlykovite, kanemite, magadiite and related phases), derive their thermodynamic and thermoelastic data, and use them in phase diagrams and micromechanical estimates of crystallization pressure. Synthesis and NMR characterization of the products close the loop with experiments.

Coarse-grained C-S-H gel

The C-S-H gel is an assembly of nanometric, flexible particles whose arrangement controls the mechanics, creep and transport of cement paste. We represent these particles as flexible multi-site objects and simulate the resulting gel mesostructure with coarse-grained simulations, to link particle shape, flexibility and packing to the properties of the gel. The same approach applies to the gels formed by the alkali–silica reaction.

C-S-H at the molecular scale

C-S-H is the main binding phase of concrete. We build and benchmark atomic models of C-S-H and use molecular simulations to compute its thermal, mechanical and transport properties, and to study the structure and dynamics of the water confined in its nanopores, including the subdiffusive water dynamics from which power-law creep emerges.

M-(A)-S-H

Magnesium silicate hydrates (M-S-H) and their aluminium-bearing form M-A-S-H form in magnesium-based and low-carbon binders and in cement exposed to magnesium-rich environments, but their structure is poorly ordered and still debated. We synthesize M-(A)-S-H and characterize it experimentally, notably by NMR, and build atomic models starting from crystalline phyllosilicates (sepiolite, talc, lizardite) and introducing defects to match the measured composition. The resulting structures are used to compute elastic, thermal and transport properties with molecular simulations, as inputs for multiscale models.

Sorption and sorption-induced deformation

Drying and wetting change the forces between the nanolayers of C-S-H, clays and other hydrates, causing shrinkage, swelling and hysteresis. We simulate sorption isotherms, disjoining pressures and cavitation in nanopores under varying humidity and temperature, and build poromechanical models that carry these effects up to the drying shrinkage and thermal deformation of concrete.

Superplasticizers (PCE)

Polycarboxylate ether (PCE) superplasticizers control the workability of modern concretes, including low-clinker mixes. We generate realistic all-atom PCE architectures and study their conformation and adsorption on cement and clay surfaces, as input to coarse-grained models of particle interactions and rheology. To build these molecules, we developed pyPCE, a Python tool that generates all-atom PCE models for LAMMPS (OPLS-AA) directly from their synthesis parameters: backbone length, grafting density, side-chain length, linkage chemistry and protonation state. An article presenting pyPCE is in preparation.

Irradiation effects in cement systems

Concrete in nuclear facilities is exposed to gamma and neutron radiation, which affects both the cement paste and the aggregates. In the hydrated paste, gamma radiation decomposes the water bound in and confined by hydrates, producing H₂; we study with molecular simulations how this hydrogen is released and transported, showing anisotropic, subdiffusive H₂ transport and the associated expansion in portlandite and other layered hydroxides. In the aggregates, neutron irradiation amorphizes the minerals and causes radiation-induced volumetric expansion (RIVE); we quantify it in quartz, feldspars, micas and inosilicates with ballistic cascade simulations using reactive force fields (ReaxFF). These results feed mesoscale simulations and simplified models of irradiated concrete.

Water and ions in clays

Swelling clays confine water and ions in nanometric interlayers. We show that their transport is subdiffusive and describe it with fractional diffusion laws, and we study how counterions control disjoining and swelling pressure. The same framework explains anomalous thermal pressurization and heat capacity in clays.

Electrical and dielectric properties

Electrical and dielectric measurements are non-destructive ways to follow hydration, moisture and degradation in concrete. We compute the dielectric permittivity and ionic conductivity of pore solutions and hydrates from molecular simulations, and upscale them with micromechanics to interpret impedance and microwave measurements on cement pastes and concrete.

Low-carbon concrete

Blended cements with limestone, slag and calcined clays are replacing clinker, but the number of possible binder combinations is too large to test experimentally. We compute the phase assemblages of commercial blended cements over time with thermodynamic modelling, and use them as input to micromechanical homogenization to predict Young's modulus and compressive strength without characterizing each binder in detail. An ongoing experimental campaign on cements from the French market measures their mechanical properties to validate and extend this binder-agnostic approach. Related work covers new hydrates such as M-(A)-S-H and the electrical response of C-S-H for non-destructive monitoring.

Micromechanics and upscaling

Micromechanics is the bridge between the molecular scale and the engineering scale in our work. We develop analytical and numerical homogenization schemes (Mori–Tanaka, self-consistent, n-coated sphere assemblages) to estimate the elastic, ageing viscoelastic, thermal, thermo-poro-elastic, transport and electrical properties of cement-based materials from their phase assemblage and microstructure. These schemes take inputs from molecular simulations and thermodynamic modelling, quantify the variability of local fields, and are compared with full-field simulations, experiments and international benchmarks.
